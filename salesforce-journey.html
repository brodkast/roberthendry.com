The Salesforceprospect journey

The short version

01

The problem

Prospects reached Salesforce from search, events and word of mouth, then met a site organised around products, not around how a business decides to buy. Who was coming to the site was unknown.

What I owned

The research and strategy behind the prospect journey: buyer personas, journey maps, the content marketing ecosystem, global navigation and the site’s feedback channel.

The long version · for the detail-inclined

Detail

01 — Strategy

Strategy

Strategy

The site is part of the product

My job was to shape how people find out about Salesforce products and services, and how they decide to buy and adopt them, across AMER, EMEA and APAC, with messaging and creative localized for each region.

We treated Salesforce’s web properties as an extension of the product, and as products in their own right. The work started before the marketing funnel and carried on past it, into support, training and customization.

02 — Research

Research

Research

Outside the Bay Area bubble

We ran as an internal agency between Product Marketing and the Digital Strategy Group: hundreds of usability studies, plus tree tests, eye tracking, competitive and brand-sentiment studies, focus groups and multi-year persona work.

The San Francisco lab was for quick usability and mobile studies only. Bay Area participants knew the jargon, so the bigger questions went to people who didn’t.

Every year at Dreamforce, the whole UX team recruited and tested among roughly 170,000 attendees, which doubled as hands-on training in low-cost research for designers and junior staff.

Research

Who the web prospect really is

One of the team’s longest-running projects defined buyer personas for three segments: very small business, mid-size and enterprise. Each had different motivations, processes, timelines and people involved.

Interviews, surveys and focus groups ran in Chicago, New York and San Francisco, and in Indianapolis and Cedar Falls, Iowa, so prospects outside big cities were represented too.

I defined what each persona had to contain, moderated the data collection, managed the researchers and set delivery expectations with stakeholders. The web prospect turned out to be three people: the Influencer, the Advocate and the Decision Maker.

Who was coming to the site was unknown, so complementary archetypes covered five business sizes, mapping the needs and pain points the sites had to address.

03 — Analysis

Analysis

Analysis

Billy’s quest to find out more

“The VP told me to research a CRM and present my top 3 choices.” Out of the research, like the personas, we developed journey maps. Billy, the Influencer, moves through motivation, discovery, awareness, education and conversion. Each stage records what he does, what he is asking, what we need to tell him and how we know it worked.

Analysis

Task Flows

I defined the major task flows, which helped prioritize development and keep the focus on high-traffic, high-value pages.

Analysis

The ecosystem, stage by stage

Mapping every touchpoint to a stage showed where the site helped and where it went silent. Each stage needed its own content, its own way in and its own measure, all held together by one navigation and one design system.

04 — Design

Design

Design

A content hub for the top of the funnel

Many first-time visitors arrived on content that had nothing directly to do with Salesforce products. I led the strategy and design of the content marketing ecosystem for them.

With SMEs, writers and stakeholders we built one section in the primary navigation, running from rich top-of-funnel content into related topics and then into product information.

Design

Information architecture: 20 navigations to one

Global navigation was my main focus as an individual contributor and later as head of research. We took 20 navigation paradigms, standardized them, then made one seamless navigation that carried on into the logged-in product.

New acquisitions kept adding whole product lines, and stakeholders kept wanting a place in the global nav. I tested the localized information architecture around the world to keep it usable.

I created the information architecture and, with the Martech product managers, engineers and content creators, wrote the requirements and acceptance criteria that drove the Agile stories.

Design

Right message, right person, right time

Personalization. AEM promised deep personalization across channels. We started with small pilots on the home page, menus and journeys. The hard part was stitching granular behaviour data to demographic and logged-in product data.

Demos. We pitched one or two R&D concepts to leadership each quarter, prototyped them and tested the winners live. One of the best was the interactive video tour.

Routing. A chatbot answered simple questions and sent support, trial and existing-product calls to the right place, so live sales agents talked to prospects.

Design

Conversion and the voice of the customer

Forms were politically sensitive, so we redesigned them last to protect the lead flow. Clear labels, descriptions, accurate photos and clear error states lifted site-wide global conversion 18% in A/B multivariate tests, and eventually all 10,000 forms moved to the design system.

I also owned the passive feedback mechanism across every site. It moved from an expensive outside vendor to a customizable Salesforce-on-Salesforce stack, which gave us a reliable voice-of-customer metric for the redesign.

Results

What Did It Do?

Results:

  • Buyer personas for 3 market segments, built from research across the country
  • Journey maps that tied every stage to the prospect’s questions, our messages and a measure of success
  • One global navigation in place of 20, continuous into the product
  • A top-of-funnel content hub, personalization pilots, an interactive demo and a routing chatbot
  • Site-wide global conversion up 18% after the forms redesign
  • A reliable voice-of-customer metric, moved in house onto Salesforce
